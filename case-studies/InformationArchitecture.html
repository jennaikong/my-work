Information Architecture Re-design

How I uncovered the tangled relationships between disjointed conversations and cases inside our member app — and streamlined navigation by rebuilding the IA of its two most-visited pages.

Overview

Project Snapshot

My Role

IA lead end to end — facilitating object mapping across care operations, clinical, and engineering, then owning task flows, prototypes, and the final design of both surfaces.

Timeline

2026–2027, in three passes: object modeling, task-flow validation, then design and phased rollout to the home page and messaging landing page.

Tools

FigJam for object mapping and flows, Figma for design, AI prototyping for clickable IA models, and unmoderated testing to compare navigation models.

Context & Problem

A structure that mirrored our systems, not the member's story

A member could have an open case with a care coordinator, a message thread with a nurse, and a past visit summary — all about the same knee. In the app, those arrived as three unrelated entries, each with its own entry point and its own vocabulary.

"Navigation problems are almost never navigation problems. Fix the model of what things are, and the menu writes itself."

The cost showed up as repetition. Members re-explained context the system already had, and care teams answered the same question in two places. The two screens carrying the most traffic — home and messaging — were the ones asking members to do the reconciling.

Three structural problems sat underneath it: conversations and cases were modeled separately with no shared parent, the same object was named differently on every surface it appeared, and home had drifted into a list of features rather than a view of what's in progress.

The Solution

One object model, two rebuilt surfaces

The breakthrough was recognizing that a conversation and a case are both evidence of the same underlying thing — an episode of care — and that members already think in episodes. Once that object existed, navigation followed from it.

Key deliverables included:

  • An object map defining every member-facing object, what it contains, and how it nests — signed off by care operations, clinical, and engineering
  • Task flows for the handful of things members actually come to do, traced against the new model to prove it held
  • A re-architected home page that leads with open episodes of care and the one action each is waiting on
  • A messaging landing page where threads and cases collapse into one list organized by episode
  • A naming convention now used in specs by teams that weren't in the room

How I Got There

Objects first, then flows, then screens

01

Object Mapping

Facilitated cross-functional sessions to name every object a member has, what it contains, and how it nests — surfacing that conversations and cases were two views of one episode of care.

02

Task Flows

Traced real member intents against the new model. Where a flow needed a step the architecture couldn't explain, the architecture was wrong — not the flow.

03

AI Prototyping

Built clickable prototypes with realistic content so the team could argue with the IA instead of debating it abstractly — and took two competing navigation models into testing rather than one.

04

Design & Rollout

Shipped the home page and messaging landing page first — the highest-traffic surfaces — so the new model had to survive real volume before the rest of the app adopted it.

Design Leadership

Making the model outlive the redesign

An IA only holds if the organization keeps speaking its language. Alongside the redesign I worked to make the object model a shared asset rather than a design artifact:

  • Ran the mapping sessions with care operations, clinical, and engineering in the same room
  • Published the object definitions and naming convention where PMs write specs
  • Coached designers on other surfaces to test their screens against the model before drawing them

Teams that weren't part of the redesign now use its vocabulary by default — which is what keeps the architecture coherent as the product grows.
